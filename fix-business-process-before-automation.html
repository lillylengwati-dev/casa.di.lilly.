Why You Should Fix a Broken Business Process Before Automating It

Automating an inefficient process does not automatically make it better. Discover why businesses should understand their workflows, remove unnecessary steps and improve operations before introducing AI or traditional automation.

Casa di Lilly Business Process Automation 13 November 2026 10–12 Min Read
START WITH THE PROCESS

Can automation fix a broken business process?

Imagine a business where customer enquiries frequently get lost, employees are unsure who should respond, information is scattered across different platforms and follow-ups happen inconsistently.

The business owner decides to introduce AI automation to solve the problem.

A new system is developed. Notifications are connected, customer messages are automated and information begins moving between applications.

But instead of making operations easier, the business now receives duplicated notifications, sends messages at the wrong time and struggles to understand which employee is responsible for each customer.

What went wrong?

The business automated a broken process. Technology made the existing workflow happen faster without fixing the underlying problem.

This is an important lesson for any organisation considering business process automation.

Before introducing AI agents, workflow automation or connected business applications, businesses should first understand how their operations actually work.

UNDERSTANDING THE PROBLEM

What is a broken business process?

A business process is a sequence of activities performed to achieve a particular outcome.

It might involve receiving a customer enquiry, preparing a quotation, confirming an appointment, processing an order or delivering a service.

A process becomes inefficient when its steps, responsibilities or information flow make it unnecessarily difficult to achieve the intended outcome.

Common signs of a broken workflow

01

Repeated manual work

Employees repeatedly enter the same information into different spreadsheets or applications because the systems are not properly connected.

02

Unclear responsibilities

Employees do not know who is responsible for responding to enquiries, approving requests or completing the next task.

03

Frequent communication delays

Work stops because employees are waiting for information, approval or updates from other people.

04

Duplicated information

Different platforms contain inconsistent customer details, order information or business records.

05

Unnecessary approval steps

Simple activities pass through too many people or stages, creating avoidable delays.

06

Frequent mistakes and rework

Information is repeatedly corrected because the process is unclear or employees do not receive the details required to complete their tasks.

07

Dependence on one person

An important process only works because one employee remembers every step. When that person is unavailable, the workflow slows down or stops.

These problems should be understood before technology is selected.

THE AUTOMATION TRAP

Why does automating a broken process often fail?

Automation follows instructions, rules and workflows. AI can add interpretation and flexibility, but neither automatically resolves poor business organisation.

If the underlying process contains unnecessary steps, unclear responsibilities or unreliable information, introducing technology without addressing those issues may reproduce the same problems.

Consider these examples.

Problem: Unclear responsibilities

Before: Multiple employees handle the same customer enquiry without knowing who owns the response.


After automating without fixing: More employees receive duplicate notifications and nobody is clearly responsible for resolving the enquiry.

Problem: Poor information management

Before: Customer details are stored inconsistently across spreadsheets and emails.


After automating without fixing: Incorrect or duplicated information is transferred into connected systems.

Problem: Unclear booking rules

Before: Employees do not have consistent rules for appointment availability.


After automating without fixing: The booking system may reproduce incorrect availability rules.

Problem: No follow-up process

Before: Nobody knows when a potential customer should receive a follow-up.


After automating without fixing: Customers receive poorly timed or repetitive follow-up messages.

Automation should improve a process, not preserve its problems in a digital format.

STEP 01 • UNDERSTAND YOUR OPERATIONS

Start with a business process audit.

Before introducing automation, take time to examine the actual steps involved in a business activity.

A process audit helps identify what happens, who is involved, which systems are used and where delays or unnecessary work occur.

Ask these questions:

  • What starts the process?
  • What information is required?
  • Who is responsible for each activity?
  • Which applications or documents are involved?
  • Where does information move between people?
  • Where do delays or mistakes regularly occur?
  • What happens when something unusual happens?
  • What does successful completion look like?

Document the process as it actually happens.

One common mistake is documenting how management believes a process works rather than how employees perform it in practice.

Speak to the people involved in completing the work. They often understand the practical difficulties, workarounds and unnecessary activities that may not be visible in formal procedures.

Process mapping is a useful way of creating a shared view of activities, decision points and hand-offs.

STEP 02 • MAP THE WORKFLOW

Map the current process before designing the new one.

Process mapping means visually or systematically documenting the steps involved in completing a business activity.

It helps business owners understand how work moves from beginning to end.

This is sometimes described as documenting the current state or "as-is" process.

Example: A customer enquiry workflow

  1. Customer submits an enquiry.
  2. Employee receives the enquiry.
  3. Customer details are manually recorded.
  4. Employee determines which service is required.
  5. Enquiry is forwarded to another person.
  6. Relevant information is checked.
  7. Quotation is prepared.
  8. Customer receives the quotation.
  9. Employee attempts to follow up.

Looking at the process in this way makes it easier to identify repeated activities and unnecessary hand-offs.

What should you record for each step?

01

The activity

What exactly happens at this stage? Describe the task in a way that another employee can understand.

02

The responsible person

Who performs the task or makes the decision?

03

The information required

What documents, customer details, approvals or records are needed?

04

The output

What should be produced or completed before the process moves forward?

05

The time and delays

How long does the activity take, and how long does the process wait before moving forward?

Mapping can reveal that the real problem is not the task itself. It may be the way information is transferred, the number of approvals required or the absence of a clear owner.

STEP 03 • FIND THE BOTTLENECKS

Identify where your business process slows down.

A bottleneck is a point in a process that restricts how quickly work can move forward.

It might be an approval that takes several days, information that must be collected manually or a single employee who is responsible for too many activities.

Common business workflow bottlenecks

01

Waiting for approvals

Tasks cannot progress because approval responsibilities are unclear or approval steps are unnecessarily complicated.

02

Searching for information

Employees spend time looking for customer details, documents or previous communication.

03

Re-entering information

Details already recorded in one application must be manually entered into another.

04

Unclear communication channels

Important information arrives through several channels without a consistent process for managing it.

05

Unnecessary decision points

Employees repeatedly need to ask questions or obtain permission for straightforward activities that could follow approved rules.

Identifying bottlenecks helps businesses decide what needs to change before selecting technology.

STEP 04 • IMPROVE THE WORKFLOW

Remove unnecessary steps before introducing automation.

Once a business understands the existing process, it can begin designing a better way of working.

This is the opportunity to simplify tasks, clarify responsibilities and remove activities that do not contribute meaningfully to the outcome.

Five practical ways to improve a process

01

Remove duplicated activities

If employees repeatedly record the same information, determine whether one reliable record can serve the entire process.

02

Standardise routine steps

Establish clear procedures for common activities so employees understand how work should be completed.

03

Clarify responsibilities

Assign ownership for each important stage of the process. Make it clear who handles the next step.

04

Simplify unnecessary approvals

Review which decisions require approval and which activities can follow already authorised business rules.

05

Improve information quality

Make sure the correct information is collected at the appropriate stage and stored consistently.

Before asking what can be automated, ask whether every step in the current process actually needs to exist.

STEP 05 • DESIGN THE BETTER PROCESS

Design the future workflow before selecting technology.

After reviewing the current process, define how the improved process should work.

This is commonly called the future-state or "to-be" process.

The future workflow should explain what happens, who is responsible, what information is required and how the process should finish.

Example: Improving the customer enquiry process

The business has reviewed its current enquiry workflow and discovered duplicate data entry, delayed follow-ups and unclear responsibilities.

After improvement, the process might look like this:

  1. Customer submits an enquiry through an approved channel.
  2. Customer information is collected in a consistent format.
  3. The enquiry is recorded in a central customer system.
  4. The correct team or employee receives responsibility.
  5. The business reviews the request and prepares a response.
  6. An approved follow-up process is triggered where appropriate.
  7. The enquiry status is updated until it is resolved.

Now the business has a clearer process that can be supported by automation.

STEP 06 • SELECT THE RIGHT AUTOMATION

Which parts of an improved business process should be automated?

Not every stage of an improved workflow needs technology.

The next step is identifying which activities are predictable enough for traditional automation, which could benefit from AI and which should remain under human control.

Traditional workflow automation

Use predefined rules when an activity has clear conditions and predictable actions.

  • Sending appointment confirmations.
  • Recording website enquiries.
  • Updating customer records.
  • Sending internal notifications.
  • Creating routine reminders.
  • Moving approved information between systems.

AI-powered automation

Consider AI when a process involves interpreting language, classifying information or generating useful responses based on context.

  • Categorising incoming customer messages.
  • Summarising business documents.
  • Extracting information from unstructured documents.
  • Preparing draft customer responses.
  • Supporting internal knowledge searches.
  • Identifying the likely purpose of enquiries.

Human-led activities

Some tasks need professional judgment, accountability or personal interaction.

  • Approving important financial decisions.
  • Handling sensitive customer complaints.
  • Making important contractual decisions.
  • Resolving exceptional business situations.
  • Approving important external commitments.

Automate

Repetitive actions with clear rules and predictable outcomes.

Use AI

Tasks requiring interpretation, classification or contextual information processing.

Keep human-led

Activities requiring judgment, accountability, professional expertise or empathy.

Combine technologies

Workflows that use AI for understanding and automation for predictable execution.

A PRACTICAL BUSINESS EXAMPLE

From a disorganised service business to a connected workflow.

Imagine a service company that receives customer enquiries through its website, email and messaging channels.

Employees manually record customer details, forward messages, prepare quotations and attempt to remember which customers need follow-ups.

The business wants to introduce AI and automation, but first needs to understand what is causing its operational problems.

Before process improvement

Scattered enquiries

Messages arrive through several channels without one consistent process for tracking them.

Duplicated records

Employees enter customer details into multiple spreadsheets and applications.

Missed follow-ups

No clear process establishes which enquiries need attention and when.

Unclear ownership

Several employees may believe someone else is responsible for responding.

After process improvement

The business establishes one central enquiry record, standard information requirements, clear responsibilities and an agreed follow-up procedure.

Only then does it introduce connected technology to support the process.

  1. Website and supported message enquiries are collected.
  2. Customer information is recorded in a central system.
  3. AI helps categorise the type of enquiry.
  4. The relevant employee receives the enquiry.
  5. Approved workflows organise reminders and follow-ups.
  6. Employees review and manage important customer interactions.
  7. Management can monitor enquiry status through organised records.

The difference is that the technology now supports a process that has a defined purpose, clear responsibilities and an organised information flow.

This is the foundation of a more useful business automation system.

STEP 07 • MEASURE THE IMPROVEMENT

How do you know whether the new process is actually better?

Introducing automation is not the end of the process improvement journey.

Businesses need to evaluate whether the changes deliver the outcome they originally wanted.

Useful business performance measures include:

01

Process completion time

How long does it take for a task to move from beginning to completion?

02

Manual effort

How much employee time is spent on repetitive activities?

03

Error frequency

How often does information need correcting or work need to be repeated?

04

Customer response time

How quickly are customer enquiries acknowledged and addressed?

05

Workflow completion rate

How many processes are successfully completed without unnecessary delays or failures?

06

Operating cost

What does the process cost to operate, including software, maintenance and employee time?

Comparing these measures before and after implementation provides a more useful picture of whether the new system is improving business operations.

WHAT BUSINESSES SHOULD AVOID

Common mistakes when automating business processes.

Choosing technology before understanding the problem

Businesses sometimes purchase software or request an AI solution before identifying what needs to improve. This can lead to unnecessary features and complicated implementations.

Ignoring employees who perform the work

Employees understand many of the practical details of everyday processes. Excluding them from the improvement process may result in a system that does not reflect how work actually happens.

Automating unnecessary activities

If a step adds no meaningful value to the outcome, consider whether it should be removed rather than automated.

Using AI without reliable information

AI systems require appropriate information and instructions. Unorganised or outdated business records can undermine the quality of the resulting workflow.

Failing to plan for unusual situations

Business processes do not always follow the normal route. Good systems should include procedures for exceptions, missing information and technical failures.

Failing to monitor the system

Businesses should regularly review automation performance and make improvements when problems appear.

RESPONSIBLE BUSINESS AUTOMATION

Why good automation needs both technology and human understanding.

A business process involves more than software and information.

It also involves people, responsibilities, customer expectations and decisions that affect how an organisation operates.

Automation should therefore be designed around the needs of the people who use the process and the people who experience its outcome.

Businesses should consider:

  • Who owns and monitors the workflow?
  • Which activities require human approval?
  • What happens when an application fails?
  • How will employees be trained to use the system?
  • How will customer information be protected?
  • How will changes to the process be managed?
  • How will performance and errors be reviewed?

In South Africa, businesses should also consider applicable privacy and information protection obligations, including POPIA, when designing connected business systems.

Good automation does not remove the need for business understanding. It depends on it.

COST & BUSINESS INVESTMENT

Does improving a process before automation save money?

Process improvement requires time and planning, but it can help businesses avoid investing in unnecessary technology or automating steps that should not exist.

It can also make requirements clearer before development begins, reducing the likelihood of repeated changes and avoidable rework.

Consider the full cost of implementation:

  • Process analysis and workflow mapping.
  • System design and planning.
  • Software and platform subscriptions.
  • Application integrations.
  • AI usage and technical infrastructure.
  • Testing and quality assurance.
  • Employee training.
  • Ongoing support and maintenance.

Businesses should compare the total investment with the value of the improved process.

A smaller, carefully designed automation can sometimes provide more value than a larger system built around an inefficient workflow.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Business process automation questions answered.

Why should you fix a business process before automating it?

Businesses should improve their processes before automation because technology follows the instructions and workflows it is given. Automating an inefficient process can reproduce unnecessary steps, duplicated work and existing errors. Improving the process first creates a clearer foundation for technology.

What is a broken business process?

A broken business process is a workflow that has inefficiencies such as unnecessary steps, unclear responsibilities, duplicated information, communication delays or frequent errors that prevent it from achieving its intended outcome effectively.

What is business process automation?

Business process automation uses technology to perform or coordinate defined activities within a business workflow. It can connect applications, transfer information, trigger actions and reduce repetitive manual tasks.

What is process mapping?

Process mapping is the documentation of activities, decisions, responsibilities and information flow within a business process. It helps organisations understand how work happens and identify areas requiring improvement.

What is the difference between an as-is and to-be process?

An as-is process describes how a business activity currently works. A to-be process describes the improved workflow that the organisation wants to achieve. Comparing them helps identify changes needed before automation is introduced.

Can AI fix inefficient business workflows?

AI can support process improvement by helping interpret information, identify patterns and perform selected tasks. However, it does not automatically resolve unclear responsibilities, poor procedures or unsuitable business rules. Those issues should be addressed as part of process improvement.

Which business processes should be automated first?

Businesses can begin by assessing repetitive, high-volume processes with clear objectives and predictable steps. Customer enquiry management, appointment scheduling, routine administration and information transfer are common opportunities.

What are the benefits of improving a workflow before automation?

Process improvement can clarify responsibilities, remove duplicated activities, reduce unnecessary steps, improve information quality and make the requirements for automation easier to define.

Is process improvement only for large businesses?

No. Small businesses can benefit from reviewing their daily workflows. Even a simple enquiry or booking process can involve unnecessary activities that can be improved before introducing automation.

Should a business use AI or traditional automation?

Traditional automation is suitable for predictable tasks with clear rules. AI is useful when activities require interpreting language, categorising information or understanding context. Many business systems combine both.

How do I know whether my business is ready for automation?

A business should understand its current processes, have reasonably reliable information, clearly defined responsibilities and measurable objectives. It should also identify who will monitor and maintain the resulting system.

THE BIGGER PICTURE

Fix the process. Then introduce the technology.

Automation can help businesses improve the way they operate, but its effectiveness depends on the quality of the underlying workflow.

Before introducing AI agents, connected applications or traditional automation, understand what currently happens, identify the unnecessary work and decide how the process should improve.

Once the workflow is organised, technology can support the activities that benefit from automation while employees retain responsibility for decisions that require human judgment.

Don't automate confusion. Build a better process, then let technology make it work smarter.
